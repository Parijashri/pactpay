Process

Before the pixels, the people.

A short writeup of how Loop's problem framing and design decisions came from actual conversations, not assumptions.

Methodology

Who I talked to

12 semi-structured interviews, 20–30 minutes each, with two groups most likely to both buy and sell second-hand locally.

7 College students in shared PGs/hostels — high item turnover, low storage space, tight budgets.
5 Young working professionals relocating within the city — furniture and appliances they can't carry on every move.

Key findings

What kept coming up

  • Everyone already uses WhatsApp groups and Instagram stories — but every single person described the same failure mode: posts get buried, "is this available?" goes unanswered, and items quietly go back into storage.
  • Trust was the #1 blocker, not price. Several students said they'd rather pay slightly more from a "verified" source than risk meeting a stranger from an anonymous post.
  • Distance mattered more than category. Most people said they'd only walk or cycle to pick something up — almost nobody was willing to travel more than 15–20 minutes for anything under ₹3,000.
  • Nobody wanted another generic marketplace app. Three people independently said OLX/Quikr "feel sketchy" — stock photos, no sense of who's on the other end.

From problem to product

Early sketches

Three rounds of low-fidelity wireframes before any visual design — testing the core idea that distance, not category, should drive the home screen.

Decisions this shaped

What changed because of it

  • The map became the centerpiece, not a secondary tab — directly responding to "distance mattered more than category."
  • Seller profiles got their own page with response time and reviews surfaced early, addressing the trust concern raised by nearly every interviewee.
  • Category filters were demoted to a secondary row beneath the map, after v2 tested poorly.
